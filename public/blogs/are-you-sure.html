“Are You Sure?”

Bruhadesh · Nov 2026 · product × psychology · 6 min read

Product people spend most of their time removing friction: one-tap checkout, one-click sign-in, fewer fields. So it feels strange to say that sometimes the best thing you can ship is a small speed bump. But sometimes it is, and the psychology explains when.

Friction is a tool, not an enemy

Friction slows people down. That's bad when they're buying something they want and good when they're about to do something they can't undo. The skill is putting the bump where the regret lives.

Why the wording matters: loss aversion

Psychologists Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky showed that losses tend to feel bigger than equivalent gains. The often-quoted estimate is roughly two to one, though it varies by person and context. Losing $10 stings more than finding $10 pleases.

That's why a vague "Are you sure?" does so little, while "This will permanently delete 214 photos" does a lot. The second one makes the loss concrete. The user sees what they're about to give up.

The failure mode: alert fatigue

Here's the catch. If you ask "Are you sure?" all the time, people stop reading and start clicking. Studies of browser security warnings have repeatedly found that many people click straight through them. A confirmation that appears every time becomes muscle memory, and muscle memory is not a safety system.

There's a better pattern for most actions: let people do it, and let them undo it. A short "Undo" toast after sending an email or archiving a thread doesn't interrupt the 99% of times it was intentional, and it rescues the 1% that wasn't.

The dark side: confirmshaming

"Are you sure you want to leave?" is also the favourite sentence of the cancel flow: guilt-trip copy ("No thanks, I don't like saving money"), hidden buttons, five screens between you and the exit. It works in the short term and corrodes trust in the long term, and regulators in several places have begun looking closely at these patterns.

A rule of thumb: friction proportional to regret

  • Low regret (liking, archiving, renaming): no friction at all.
  • Medium regret (sending, moving): no dialog, but a visible undo.
  • High and irreversible (deleting data, paying, publishing): confirm, and be specific about the consequence. Some tools even ask you to type the name of what you're deleting, which forces a moment of real attention.

The goal of friction isn't to stop people. It's to give a hurried brain one honest chance to catch up with its own hand.
