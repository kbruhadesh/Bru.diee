The Progress Bar Lies (and We Love It)

Bruhadesh · Nov 2026 · product × psychology · 6 min read

Nobody has ever timed a progress bar with a stopwatch. They feel it. And what they feel has surprisingly little to do with how many seconds it actually took. That gap between real time and felt time is one of the most useful ideas in product design.

Time is felt, not measured

Usability researchers have long used three rough thresholds for response time: about 0.1 seconds feels instant, about 1 second keeps your train of thought unbroken, and about 10 seconds is the limit of holding your attention on the task. Beyond that, people drift to another tab and may not come back.

A good loading experience is therefore less about being fast and more about managing which of those thresholds you're in, and what the user's mind is doing while it waits.

The labor illusion: we value visible effort

In a well-known study, Ryan Buell and Michael Norton found that people rated online services more highly when the site showed the work being done, such as "searching airlines…", even when that made them wait longer than an instant result would have. We treat visible effort as a sign of value.

That's why travel sites often show a "checking 400 options" screen. Sometimes the results are ready sooner, and the screen exists because instant answers can feel suspiciously cheap.

The ethical edge: showing real work builds trust, but staging fake work can backfire badly once people notice. Use it to explain, not to deceive.

Shapes that make waiting feel shorter

  • Skeleton screens show the outline of what's coming, so the wait feels like it's already in progress. The research evidence is mixed, but they're popular for a reason.
  • Progress bars that finish strong. Researchers studying perceived duration have found that bars which speed up towards the end feel faster than steady ones.
  • Something to do. A tip, a preview, a task. Occupied time is felt as shorter than empty time.

The peak-end rule

Kahneman and colleagues ran a famous experiment where people held a hand in painfully cold water. In one version they endured the cold a bit longer, but the final stretch was slightly less cold. Afterwards, many preferred repeating the longer trial. People don't remember experiences as averages. They remember the peak and the end.

People don't rate how long it took. They rate how it felt at the worst and last moments.

What this means when you ship

  • Fix the worst moment of the flow first: the slowest screen, the most confusing step.
  • Treat the ending as a feature. A clear, calm success state beats a long flow that just stops.
  • Keep the last stretch fast and the wording reassuring.
  • Tell the truth about what's happening. Smooth the experience, don't fake the outcome.

Performance has two layers: how fast the system is, and how fast it feels. Engineers own the first one. Product design owns the second, and users only ever experience the second.
